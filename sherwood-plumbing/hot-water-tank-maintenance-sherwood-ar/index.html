    Relief Valve and Expansion Tank Testing in Sherwood

    Street pressure over 80 psi is hard on every fixture in the house, and plumbing codes generally call for a pressure reducing valve above that number. A gauge on a hose bib tells us the static pressure. The relief valve lever gets lifted next. If the valve opens and reseats cleanly and the expansion tank holds its charge, the tank is safe from pressure spikes.

    Yearly Maintenance Plans in Sherwood, AR

    A maintenance plan puts your tank on the calendar so nobody has to remember. We call you when it is due. Each plan is a fixed yearly rate for one or two visits, priority booking and a parts discount. Homes on well water or very hard water usually do better with the twice-a-year option.

    Burner and Pilot Service in Sherwood, AR

    On a gas tank, a dirty burner throws a lazy yellow flame and leaves soot on the flue baffle. The burner comes out and gets brushed and vacuumed. We also check the thermocouple or flame sensor, since a weak one lets the pilot go out every few days and leaves you resetting it on your knees. The goal is a steady blue flame.

    Rheem and Bradford White Tank Service in Sherwood

    Rheem, Bradford White, AO Smith and State make most of the tanks in local garages, and each has quirks a tech should know before opening a drain valve. On some models the anode sits under a plastic top cap. Bradford White often uses a combo anode in the hot outlet nipple. We carry the right sockets for each brand.

    Electric Element Check in Sherwood

    Electric tanks have two elements, usually 4,500 watts at 240 volts, and the lower one does most of the work and takes most of the scale. Each element gets an ohm reading. If the hot water quits halfway through a shower, the lower element is the first suspect. We replace it with the matching wattage and voltage.

    Expansion Tank Service in Sherwood, AR

    A thermal expansion tank is the small steel tank on the cold line above the heater, and it gives heated water somewhere to go when a check valve or pressure reducer closes the system. We test the air side with a tire gauge. Precharge should match your house water pressure. A waterlogged expansion tank sounds solid when you knock on it, and it lets pressure spike every time the burner fires.

    Maintenance Versus Replacement Costs in Sherwood, AR

    A yearly flush costs a small fraction of a new installed tank, which often runs $1,400 to $3,000 or more depending on size and venting. We tell you which one makes sense. A tank over 10 to 12 years old with rust at the base is a replacement. Younger tanks with a sound liner are worth maintaining.

    Electric Thermostat Service in Sherwood

    Each element on an electric tank has its own thermostat, and the upper one carries a reset button for the high-limit switch. When the reset trips, something caused it. We trace the cause, often a shorted element or a stuck thermostat, before resetting it. Power stays off at the breaker the whole time.

    Frequently Asked Questions About Hot Water Tank Maintenance in Sherwood

    Expect $75 to $200 on top of the flush for a new anode, parts and labor together. Powered anodes cost more. Seized rods and flexible link rods land near the high end.

    About an hour for a typical 40 or 50 gallon tank, sometimes up to two. Heavy scale stretches it out. A tank that has not been flushed in years can take four or five drain and refill cycles before the hose runs clear, and a seized anode adds another 15 to 30 minutes.

    Yearly, for most homes. Every six months is better on very hard water. Manufacturers like Rheem and Bradford White list annual flushing in their owner manuals, and a dated service record helps if you ever file a sediment-related warranty claim.

    Popping or rumbling, hot water that runs out sooner than it used to, rusty or cloudy water from the hot side, a sulfur smell, or moisture around the base. One of those is enough. A dripping relief valve pipe counts too.

    Water chemistry decides how fast the anode gets eaten. Softened water is the hardest on rods. Sodium from a softener makes the water more conductive, so a magnesium rod can be gone in two or three years instead of five, which is why we check it every visit.

    Sediment hardens, the burner or elements work harder, and the bottom of the tank runs hotter than it should. You lose efficiency first. After that the anode runs out, corrosion starts on the liner, and the usual ending is a leak from the bottom seam on a tank that might have lasted another five years.

    Rumbling is the deeper cousin of popping, and it means a thick bed of sediment is shifting and boiling on the bottom of the tank each time the burner lights. It gets louder over time. Electric tanks rumble less, but they crust up the lower element instead.
