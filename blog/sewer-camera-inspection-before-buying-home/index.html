    Why a Sewer Camera Inspection Belongs on Every Arkansas Home Inspection

    By Andrew Blake · August 13, 2026 · 5 min read

    What a Home Inspection Does Not Cover

    A general home inspector runs water in the sinks, flushes toilets and checks for leaks under cabinets. Most standard inspections never put a camera in the main drain. That line runs from the house to the city main or the septic tank, often 40 to 100 feet underground, and if it is broken the repair can cost more than a new roof, which lands on the new owner the day the deed records. The scope takes about an hour.

    Running water for ten minutes proves very little. A line with a crack or a root mat can still drain a sink just fine. Problems show up once a family of four starts doing laundry and taking showers every day, usually in the first few months after move-in. Vacant houses hide it best.

    Common Sewer Line Problems in Arkansas Homes

    Homes here are split between crawlspace and slab construction, and crawlspaces allow more direct access for repairs and repiping.

    Roots are the most common thing we find on a pre-purchase scope. They enter at joints in clay and cast iron and grow into mats that catch paper. Bellies, sections that have sagged and hold water, are next, followed by offset joints where one piece of pipe has shifted out of line with the next one. The camera picks up every one of them.

    Grease and scale show up on older lines as well. A thick coat narrows the pipe and catches paper at every joint. A seller who had the line cleaned the week before listing can make a bad pipe look better on paper, so ask when the last cleaning was and read the video for fresh cutter marks on the walls. Fresh scrape marks tell a story.

    Newer homes across Arkansas mostly use PEX and PVC, which resist corrosion but still need proper support and freeze protection.

    The age of the house tells you what pipe to expect. Homes built before the 1980s often have cast iron or clay laterals, and some have Orangeburg. Cast iron in its fifties and sixties commonly shows heavy scale or channel rot, where the floor of the pipe has worn through, while PVC from newer builds usually looks clean unless it was bedded poorly. Request records of earlier repairs from the seller.

    What Skipping It Can Cost

    Sewer line replacement is one of the most expensive repairs a house can need, right alongside a foundation or a roof. A line that runs under a driveway or a big tree costs more again. Trenchless lining or bursting often brings the price down and saves the yard, but nobody wants to learn about a collapsed lateral two weeks after closing, when the seller is no longer part of the conversation. Before closing, a bad line is something you can negotiate.

    A camera report gives you options. Sellers can be asked for a repair or a closing credit. Homeowner policies generally exclude gradual deterioration and root damage to a sewer line, so the cost lands on the buyer if nobody looks before the papers are signed. Walking away is an option too.

    Septic homes need an extra look. A scope down the outlet line shows the tank inlet and the condition of the baffle. A full septic inspection with a pump-out is a separate service, but the camera pass tells you whether the pipe between house and tank is sound before you spend money on the tank itself. Rural listings deserve both.

    When to Call a Professional

    Book the scope during your inspection window, ideally in the first few days so there is time to get a repair quote if the video shows trouble. The plumber runs the camera from a cleanout or pulls a toilet if there is no cleanout. DGR Plumbing records the full run, marks the footage and depth of any defect with a locator, and sends you the video with a written summary you can hand to the seller, and the scheduling line is (501) 251-9708. Bring your agent in early.

    What a Good Camera Inspection Includes

    Look for a report with video, not a pass or fail checkbox. The camera should travel the whole line, cleanout to main or tank. Footage markers and a locator reading at any problem spot let a second plumber or the seller's contractor quote the same repair without rescoping, which saves days during a tight closing schedule. Keep the file after you move in.

    Owners of older homes can use a scope too. Running a camera every few years on an older line spots root intrusion early. If the house has had two or more main line backups in a year, the line almost always has a defect worth seeing on video, and cleaning it again without looking just resets the clock. Look first, then choose the fix.
