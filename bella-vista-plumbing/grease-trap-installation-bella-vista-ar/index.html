    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Installation in Bella Vista

    Small under-sink hydromechanical traps usually land between $1,000 and $3,000 installed, and buried interceptors with excavation commonly run $5,000 to $20,000 or more. The spread comes from access and how much concrete or paving we cut. Permits add a little. We quote in writing after a site visit, with the trap, the labor and the permit fees listed as separate lines.

    For indoor traps we lean on Zurn, Watts, Schier and Endura, because their parts are easy to find and their units carry PDI or ASME certification. Schier's plastic bodies do not rust, which helps in hot, wet kitchens. Brand matters less than sizing. A well-known trap installed too small will still fail inspection, so we pick the size first and the brand second.

    Nearly every food business needs one. Plumbing codes require a grease interceptor wherever fixtures discharge grease-laden waste, which covers restaurants, bakeries and most food prep spaces. A few low-grease operations get waived, and the sewer authority makes that call. Ask before you sign the lease.

    The common rule is to clean before grease and solids fill 25 percent of the trap's depth. Busy kitchens with small indoor traps often hit that mark every week. Outdoor tanks go longer. A gravity interceptor typically gets pumped monthly to quarterly, and some sewer programs set the schedule for you.

    We start with a fixture list and the drain rate of each fixture. Sinks are calculated by compartment volume and drain-down time, typically one or two minutes, and dish machines by the manufacturer's discharge rating. That gives the GPM figure. Hydromechanical traps are picked by that GPM rating, while gravity interceptors are sized by volume and retention time from the same data.

    That depends on the code your jurisdiction follows and the type of trap. Some programs require dish machines to drain through the interceptor, while others keep high-temperature discharge away from certain hydromechanical units because hot water and detergent can push emulsified grease through. We check before we pipe it. Food waste disposers are a separate question, and most codes do not allow a disposer to discharge into a grease interceptor at all.

    It keeps cooking grease out of the public sewer. Hot greasy water cools as it moves through the trap, the grease floats to the top, and cleaner water leaves through the outlet. Grease that reaches the mains hardens into blockages, and the sewer authority bills the kitchens it traces back to. Your own drain lines benefit too.

    An under-sink hydromechanical trap with good access is usually a half-day job, including the flow control and a leak test. Floor-recessed units take longer because we cut and patch concrete.
